Tasting the Training Slice

Every page so far was handed a finished map. This one makes the map. A classifier looks at a noisy photo of the pizza and learns from spots a taster has already checked. The question is how to grade it. Hold back some of the tasted spots and test on those, the usual move, and you can grade yourself far too kindly: if the hold-outs sit right next to spots the classifier learned from, it is tasting its own training slice. Section 2.7 of the paper says training and test data must be independent, and that when reference data come in clusters, whole clusters must go one way or the other. Here the 64 squares are the clusters.

Step 1 · A photo of the pizza

The classifier never sees the real pizza, only this photo. Two things make a spot resemble its neighbours. The first is the pizza itself: toppings come in patches, so a spot's neighbours almost always have its topping. That is already true of the real pizza on the left, and it is what the leak on this page runs on. The second is the bake: an oven browns one side more than the other and chars a patch here and there, so neighbouring spots share a tint whatever their topping. Grain, speckle on each spot alone, works the other way and hides the toppings. Mushroom and anchovy look alike, as on every page.

Speckle on every spot, each spot on its own.

Browning shared by neighbouring spots, the oven's hot side and a few charred patches. It makes neighbours alike in colour, on top of the toppings already making them alike in kind. It matters most to a classifier that goes by colour alone.

Step 2 · Split the tasted spots two ways

The taster checks some squares, a few bites in each: a two-stage sample, like real reference data that come in image chips. Half the tasted spots train the classifier and the other half grade it. The split is made two ways, from the same spots.

Step 4 · The paper's formula, and the one it doesn't give

The paper has no equation for a hold-out score, because it is not an estimator of anything about the whole map. The claimed number is just the share of test spots the map gets right. The fresh sample uses the paper's stratified estimator, Eq. 13 (p. 11), with the strata built from the classifier's own map. Sec. 2.7 (p. 8) says building strata from the map does not break independence. Both cards follow the split by spot map.

What Section 2.7 says

Don't grade on the training data

Using the same data for training and validation gives an optimistically biased accuracy (Hammond and Verbyla 1996, cited on p. 7). Tasting the training slice is the extreme case: a nearest-neighbour classifier gets every training spot right, because its nearest neighbour is itself. Splitting by spot is the mild case of the same thing. The test spot is not in the training set, but its neighbours are, and on a pizza the neighbours have the same topping. Picture the extreme, a pizza with one topping on each quarter: a classifier that only knows where it is passes any by-spot test with full marks while having learned nothing but the four quarters. The fix is the same one the paper gives, which is to hold out whole slices.

a test spot next to a training spot is a training spot in disguise

Training and testing want different samples

Training can use purposive, convenient sites. Accuracy assessment needs a probability sample (p. 8). Independence is easiest with a separate probability sample drawn for the assessment, and drawing it after the map exists is fine: strata built from the map change precision, not bias (p. 8). Step 3's fresh sample does exactly that.

When one sample must serve both

If the reference data come from one probability sample and are then divided, divide at the cluster level. A cluster assigned to training takes all its spots with it (p. 8). Splitting the spots inside a cluster between training and testing violates independence, and several studies found optimistic accuracies from it (Friedl et al. 2000; Zhen et al. 2013; Lyons et al. 2018).

Honesty notes

The split by square can read a little low. Its test squares are ones the classifier never learned from, while its real accuracy also counts the training squares, where it does best. The fresh sample has no such lean, which is the paper's point. With tasted spots in all 64 squares the by-spot inflation shrinks: the training spots then cover the whole pizza, so a test spot is no closer to them than any other spot is. The inflation is about clustered reference data, which is what Sec. 2.7 is about. This classifier is a toy, and the size of the gap depends mostly on how much it leans on position, because position is how it borrows a neighbour's topping. The uneven bake matters much less than Step 1 might suggest: at the defaults, taking it from 0 to 100% moves the by-spot gap by about half a point (14.8 to 15.4 over 20 tastings, ours). It only does the leaking on its own for the colour-only classifier, where the gap goes from about 1 point to 2 as the bake gets more uneven, and it never reaches what position does. Tidier toppings don't make the leak worse either: with the patches from 05's clumping dial at full, the position-and-colour map is right more often everywhere and the gap shrinks to about 10 points. The leak is largest when the toppings are messy at the scale between a neighbour and a square, which is the scale the paper pizza is messy at.
