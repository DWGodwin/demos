How Much Anchovy Is There?

Count the anchovy spots on the map and you get 5.0% of the pizza. That's pixel counting, and it's quick. The paper's Table 5 says 7.4%, with a standard error of 1.9. The map undercounts because it misses more anchovy than it invents. Section 4.3 says area should come from the taster's sample, and the map's job is to make that estimate more precise. This page takes the map's count apart, estimates area from a sample, and asks when the map's count is good enough.

The map's count, fixed with its own mistakes

The real share of a topping is the map's share, minus the spots the map wrongly calls that topping (commission), plus the spots of that topping the map called something else (omission). This is the paper's Eq. 21.

p+4=5.0%−1.8%+4.2%=7.4%

Step 3 · One random sample, read three ways

Now the taster picks spots at random from the whole pizza, without looking at the map. There are three ways to turn those tastes into an area. Ignore the map: the share of tastes that were this topping. Eq. 22: the map's count, corrected by the gap between what the taster found and what the map said at the same spots. Eq. 19: weight the tastes by map topping, which the paper calls poststratification (Sec. 4.2). All three use the same tastes.

Step 4 · Pixel counting or the sample?

Pixel counting has no sampling error but a fixed bias. The sample has no bias but a standard error that shrinks as it grows. Eq. 23 puts both on one scale: mean square error is variance plus bias squared. The chart shows its square root, in points, so it reads like a standard error. The sample is stratified with the same number of tastes per topping, 500 repeats per point.

Taster slips

A slip: the taster names the wrong topping, one of the other three at random. Section 4.3.2 says reference errors are the only source of bias for the sample. Explainer 07 is all about them.

What Section 4.3 says

Area comes from the sample

The map's count is quick, but classification errors bias it (Eq. 5). Following Olofsson et al. (2014), the paper says to estimate area from the reference sample, because the taster's verdict is the best account of what is really on the pizza (Sec. 4.3).

The map's job is precision

The map still matters. It supplies the strata, for the design or afterwards, and the more accurate the map, the smaller the standard errors of the area estimates (Sec. 4.3.1).

the sample gives the area, the map shrinks its error

"Bias-adjusted" is a misleading name

Eq. 21 makes it tempting to think of the sample as a patch on the map's count. The paper says that framing puts the map in the wrong role: the sample supplies the data and the map is extra information. Eq. 22 matches Eq. 19 only for a stratified random sample, and the paper prefers Eq. 19 for its smaller standard error (Stehman 2013).

When pixel counting could win

Mean square error is variance plus bias squared (Eq. 23). Pixel counting's error is the map's bias. The sample's is its standard error, plus any bias from a wrong taster (Sec. 4.3.2). The paper says it is unclear what variance a map should carry; this page counts none, though a different training set would give a different map.

Report both

Report area as a percent of the study area with its standard error, next to the map's own shares (Sec. 4.4). Ideally the map would be adjusted to match the sample's area, which the paper leaves as an open problem.
