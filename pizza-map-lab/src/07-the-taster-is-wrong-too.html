The Taster Is Wrong Too

Every page so far trusted the taster. When the taster said anchovy, it was anchovy. Real reference data are another classification, and they make mistakes too (Sec. 6). Foody (2013) worked out what that does to a rare class. A taster who is right 95% of the time, checking a map that is right 90% of the time, makes a 1% topping look nearly six times bigger. This page uses a pizza with only two toppings, anchovy and everything else, so you can set how rare the anchovy is.

Foody's equations

Foody (2013, p. 786) rewrites Gart and Buck's (1966) equations for the apparent accuracy and area. T is the truth, R the reference (our taster) and C the classification (our map). Subscript t is the target class, anchovy, and o is the other class, the rest.

Foody Eq. 4 · apparent areano map in it
E^{A} = E\,(P_t^{\mathrm{RvT}} + P_o^{\mathrm{RvT}} - 1) - P_o^{\mathrm{RvT}} + 1
E^{A} = \id{q4-e}{0.010} \times (\id{q4-rt}{0.95} + \id{q4-ro}{0.95} - 1) - \id{q4-ro2}{0.95} + 1 = \id{q4-v}{0.059}

Rearranged, it says the taster finds the real anchovy it catches plus every other spot it wrongly calls anchovy:

E^{A} = E\,P_t^{\mathrm{RvT}} + (1-E)(1-P_o^{\mathrm{RvT}}) = \id{q4-a}{0.0095} + \id{q4-b}{0.0495}

The map's accuracy, P^{\mathrm{CvT}}, appears nowhere. A perfect map gets the same wrong area. When anchovy is rare, the second term is almost all of it.

The taster's false anchovy land in the map's "rest" row, so the map looks like it misses most of the anchovy.

Foody Eq. 3 · user'sanchovy
U_t = \frac{E\,P_t}{E\,P_t + (1-P_o)(1-E)}
U_t^{\mathrm{CvR}} = \frac{\id{q3-a}{0.0254}}{\id{q3-a2}{0.0254} + \id{q3-b}{0.1796}} = \id{q3-v}{12.4}\%, \qquad U_t^{\mathrm{CvT}} = \id{q3-t}{3.4}\%

Foody uses the same equation for the true and apparent values, with E^{A} and the apparent producer's accuracies (Eqs. 1–2) put in for the apparent one.

Foody's Fig. 1(c) prints 23.73% and 79.88%. His Eqs. 1 and 2 give 23.738% and 79.888%, so the figure cuts the digits off rather than rounding. His text also says a 99%/99% taster gives 1.89%; his Eq. 4 gives 1.98%, which fits his own "close to double".

What Section 6 says

The reference is just another classification

Accuracy assessment assumes the reference labels are the truth. In reality they are another classification, and they can be wrong (Sec. 6, p. 16). Every disagreement gets blamed on the map, even when the taster made the mistake. The paper names three problems: wrong labels (6.1), spots one label can't describe (6.2), and interpreters who disagree (6.3).

Small taster errors, big area errors

Even small reference error can bias accuracy and area a lot, shrinking a class's apparent accuracy and inflating its area (Sec. 6.1, pp. 16–17). Foody's "nearly a factor of 6" is Scenario I; his "nearly 40" is Scenario II at 0.5%. Reference error also makes accuracy depend on how common the class is.

rare class + slightly wrong taster = much too much anchovy

What to do about it

If the taster's accuracy is known, the real accuracy can be worked out (Sec. 6.1, citing Staquet et al. 1981 and Foody 2010). Step 4's corrected row does that for area. With several labels per spot but no known accuracy, latent class analysis can estimate it (Foody 2012).

Design-based inference assumes the labels are right

The standard errors on every page of this lab only count sampling error (Sec. 5, pp. 15–16). Taster error and taster disagreement need measurement error models on top. That's why Steps 4 and 5 show formula SEs that miss the real problem.

Mixed spots

Report how many spots were ambiguous and whether they were used (Sec. 6.2, p. 17). Matching the primary label alone is close to the fuzzy MAX operator; matching the primary or the alternate is close to RIGHT (Gopal and Woodcock 1994; Stehman et al. 2003).
