Normalizing and Kappa

Two habits left over from the days when an error matrix was treated like any other two-way table (Sec. 1.1). Normalizing rescales the matrix until every topping is a quarter of the pizza, which describes a pizza nobody baked. Kappa subtracts "chance agreement" from overall accuracy, using a model of chance that nobody can point to on the pizza, and then moves almost in lockstep with overall accuracy anyway. The paper lists both, with the 85% benchmark, as bad practice to drop (Sec. 7.5). This page lets you do all three to the paper pizza and watch what breaks.

What normalizing does to the paper's equations

The paper gives no equation for normalizing, only the result: every row and column total forced to 1/c, and user's accuracy made equal to producer's (Sec. 4.1, p. 10). Here is the rescaling we run, and what it does to Eqs. 3 and 4 for anchovy.

The rescalingnot an equation in the paper
p_{ij} \leftarrow \frac{p_{ij}}{c\,p_{i+}} \quad\text{then}\quad p_{ij} \leftarrow \frac{p_{ij}}{c\,p_{+j}}, \qquad c = 4

Rows first, then columns, over and over (iterative proportional fitting). Each pass undoes a little of the previous one, so it takes about 20 passes to settle here. After 0 passes the worst total is 30.4 points away from 25.

after normalizingp4+ = p+4 = 1/c
U_4 = P_4 = \frac{p_{44}}{1/c} = \frac{\id{qn-pii3}{18.0}}{\id{qn-q}{25.0}} = \id{qn-np}{72.0}\%

Once both totals are 1/c, the two accuracies share a denominator and can't differ. The map says anchovy and is right 64% of the time; it finds 43% of the real anchovy. The normalized matrix says 72% for both, a number that describes neither.

Step 3 · Kappa and the chance it corrects for

Kappa (Eq. 24) takes overall accuracy and subtracts the agreement a map would get "by chance", then rescales so a perfect map scores 1. For Table 5 that chance agreement is 41.3% and kappa is 0.653 (ours; the paper prints no kappa).

But which chance? Eq. 24 uses one model: shuffle the map's labels among the spots, keeping the map's shares. Other models are just as reasonable and give other answers (Sec. 4.8: chance agreement is a model construct). Pick one and shuffle.

Chance model

Eq. 24 · kappapopulation parameter
\kappa = \frac{O - \sum_{i=1}^{c} p_{i+}\,p_{+i}}{1 - \sum_{i=1}^{c} p_{i+}\,p_{+i}}
\kappa = \frac{\id{q24-o}{0.796} - \id{q24-e}{0.413}}{1 - \id{q24-e2}{0.413}} = \id{q24-v}{0.653}

O is overall accuracy (Eq. 2). The sum is the chance agreement: the share of the pizza a shuffled map would get right, on average. Kappa is a straight-line function of O once that sum is fixed, which is why Sec. 4.8 calls reporting both redundant.

Map share times taster share, topping by topping. Cheese alone supplies 33.2 of the 41.3 points, because a shuffled label lands on cheese most of the time and is cheese most of the time. No actual spot on the pizza is "right by chance" (Sec. 4.8).

Step 5 · Does it pass 85%?

The 85% benchmark comes from Anderson (1971), who set it for large-area maps of broad land cover classes. It has no universal status (Sec. 1.1 and 7.5, pp. 3 and 19). A target should be set for the application before the analysis, and then the question is whether the estimate and its interval clear it. What the paper says

Where these habits came from

Early accuracy assessment borrowed the statistics of two-way contingency tables (Congalton et al. 1983). The paper calls the normalized matrix and kappa two unfortunate remnants of that borrowing, both now recommended against (Sec. 1.1, p. 3).

Normalizing fails the map relevant criterion

An error matrix should reflect how much of the region each class really covers, in both the map and the reference (criterion 1, Sec. 1.2). Forcing every total to 1/c gives a matrix with no basis in the map or the ground, since classes are rarely equally common, and it makes user's equal producer's, which is almost never true of a real map (Sec. 4.1, p. 10; Stehman 2004). The same criterion is broken by reporting raw sample counts from an equal-allocation stratified sample, which is explainer 02's Table 4 story.

normalized matrix = a pizza nobody baked

Kappa

Chance agreement is a model construct; different models give different chance agreement, and no actual pixel can be identified as right by chance. A random classification is not a realistic alternative way to make a map (Ye et al. 2018). Kappa is highly correlated with overall accuracy (Liu et al. 2007), so reporting both is redundant, and chance correction is rarely carried through to the class level (conditional kappa). Pontius and Millones (2011) found it rarely if ever changed an assessment's conclusion. The paper's verdict: kappa is to accuracy assessment what the appendix is to the body (Sec. 4.8, p. 15).

Three things to stop doing

Sec. 7.5 (p. 19) names three widespread bad practices: an unjustified 85% target, normalizing the error matrix, and correcting for chance agreement. Correcting for chance may help when judging a classifier, but has no place in assessing a map. Targets should be set in advance and fit the purpose.
