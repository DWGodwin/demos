The Sampling Design Lab

In explainer 02 the taster picked 25 spots from each topping on the map. That's one sampling design. Here you can try six, plus one that cheats, and repeat each a thousand times. Section 2 of the paper asks one thing of every design: each spot on the pizza must have a known chance of being tasted, and that chance must be above zero.

What this sample says

Repeat every design 1,000 times

One sample can be lucky. Here every design tastes the pizza 1,000 times at the size you picked. A good design centres on the dashed truth line. A tighter pile means more trust in any single sample.

Spread is the standard deviation of the 1,000 answers: the real sampling error. Formula SE is the standard error each sample reports for itself, averaged. "Worth" turns the spread into the number of simple random tastes with the same spread.

The paper's formulas

Section 4.2 asks for "consistent" estimators: formulas that match the design. Every taste gets a weight of one over its chance of being picked. For simple random, systematic and stratified samples, that leads to the stratified formulas (Eqs. 12–18). Cluster samples need formulas of their own. These cards follow this sample. Check against the paper

Equal strata at 100 tastes is the paper's design: 25 per topping on the map (Table 4). Its 1,000 answers should centre on Table 5's numbers, and spread about as much as Table 5's standard errors say.

Table 4 in, Table 5 out

Running Eqs. 16–18 and 20 on Table 4's counts gives every standard error in Table 5 exactly, when each stratum divides by n_{i+} - 1. Eq. 20 is printed with n_{i+}, which gives area standard errors of 4.8, 4.5, 2.5 and 1.8 instead of Table 5's 4.9, 4.6, 2.6 and 1.9. This page uses n_{i+} - 1 everywhere.

What Section 2 says

Every spot needs a known chance above zero

That's what makes a probability sample (Sec. 2.1). The chances don't have to be equal. The convenience taster breaks the rule: they only grab the edge pieces, the ones with crust, so the middle of the pizza can never be tasted. Toppings thin out toward the crust, so the edge pieces are mostly cheese, where the map is usually right. No formula can weight the middle back in, because its chance was zero.

weight = 1 ÷ chance of being tasted

The sample doesn't have to look like the pizza

The paper calls "the sample must represent the population" a myth (Sec. 2.1). Pick equal strata and look at the two bars beside the pizza: anchovy gets a quarter of the tastes but covers 5% of the map. The estimates still centre on the truth, because each taste is weighted by its chance.

Stratify for objectives, cluster for costs

Section 2.2's rule of thumb. Strata by map class guarantee enough tastes of a rare topping to pin down its user's accuracy. Switch the chart to user's accuracy for anchovy and compare equal strata with simple random. Clusters are cheaper to reach, but neighbouring spots tend to agree, so a block is worth only a few separate tastes.

Proportional strata gain little
