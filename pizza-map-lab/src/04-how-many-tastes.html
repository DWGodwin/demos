How Many Tastes?

The paper's taster in explainer 02 took 25 tastes of each topping. Is that enough? Section 2.5 gives a formula for planning the number before you start (Eq. 1). After tasting, the standard errors answer the question. Here you can plan a sample size, taste the pizza at that size, and check how often the 95% intervals really catch the truth.

Step 4 · Count, not percent

Section 2.5 ends on this: the number of tastes sets the precision, not the share of the pizza tasted. Compare this pizza's map-anchovy spots with a bottomless pizza that has the same mix but never runs out. Tasting with replacement is the same as tasting a pizza so big you never hit the same spot twice, so 0% of it gets eaten.

What Section 2.5 says

Planning is a forecast

The formula is exact, but p is a guess. The paper suggests trying several values of p. The biggest n comes at p = 50%, so that gives an upper bound. In practice the budget often sets the size, and Eq. 1 forecasts whether it will be enough.

n = z² p (1 − p) ÷ d²

Pick d by importance

A priority class can get a narrower interval (d = 2 points) and a less important one a wider one (d = 5). Eq. 1 plans overall accuracy for a simple random sample, or each topping's user's accuracy for a stratified one. It can't plan producer's accuracy, because the true toppings aren't known until you taste.

Was it big enough? Look at the standard errors

After tasting, the planning details matter less than the standard errors you got (Sec. 2.5). Criterion 4 in Sec. 1.2 asks for reliable results, shown by small standard errors. If they're too wide, add more tastes.

Hay's 50 per class

Hay (1979) proposed at least 50 per class, and many studies still use it (Sec. 1.1). At p = 80% that gives an interval of about ± 11 points. Whether that's enough depends on what the map is for.

Small samples lean

In explainer 02, producer's accuracy for pepperoni came out a little high at 25 per topping. Pick producer's accuracy for pepperoni in Step 3: the lean shrinks as the tastes grow, and the intervals catch the truth less often at small sizes.
