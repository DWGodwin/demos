Twenty-Five of Each

In explainer 01 we checked every spot on the pizza. A real taster can't eat the whole pizza, so they taste a sample. To make sure the rare anchovy gets tasted at all, they taste the same number of spots from each topping the map shows. That's a good plan, and it's exactly the design behind the paper's Table 4. It also means the sample looks nothing like the pizza, and counting it as if it did describes a different pizza.

The taster picks this many spots at random from each topping on the map. The paper's example uses 25.

What the sample says

Two ways to turn the same tastes into accuracy numbers. Raw counts treat every taste the same. Weighted lets each taste speak for the share of the pizza it came from. The dashed column is the truth, from checking every spot, and it matches the paper's Table 5.

Step 1: count the tastes

Rows are what the map said, columns are what the taster found. Every row adds to the same number because that's how the sample was drawn. This is the paper's Table 4 layout.

Step 2: weight by area

Each row is rescaled so it adds up to that topping's share of the map. Now the cells are shares of the whole pizza, and the row totals look like the real map again. This is the paper's Table 5 layout.

Step 3: the paper's formulas

Steps 1 and 2 are the estimators from the paper's Section 4.2. Here they are with this sample's numbers filled in. A hat, as in pˆ, means "estimated from the sample". Classes are numbered 1 to 4 as in the paper's Table 3. Click any cell in either matrix to follow that cell, its row and its column.

A row's estimated total pˆi+ is just Wi, so the weight cancels. That's why raw and weighted user's accuracy always agree.

Why weighting works

A cheese spot on the map has a tiny chance of being tasted, because there are so many of them. An anchovy spot on the map has a much bigger chance. So each tasted cheese spot is standing in for hundreds of untasted ones, and each tasted anchovy spot stands in for far fewer.

weight = 1 ÷ chance of being tasted

Weighting by the map's share puts that back. In the paper this is Eq. 12: each cell becomes the map share times the row's sample fraction. Step 3 works it through for any cell.

User's accuracy doesn't care

User's accuracy only uses one row, and every taste in a row had the same chance of being picked. So raw and weighted give the same answer. Producer's and overall accuracy mix rows together, so they need the weights. Eq. 14 in Step 3 shows the weight cancelling.

A quick smell test

If someone shows you a matrix where every row adds up to the same number, ask whether the sample was 25 of each. If it was, the raw producer's and overall accuracies are probably wrong (Section 4.4).
