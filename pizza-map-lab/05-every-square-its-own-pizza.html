Every Square Its Own Pizza

Toppings pile up, and a classifier's mistakes pile up too: under a glare on the photo, every spot can come out wrong. Neighbouring spots agree with each other. Section 2.4 says this never biases a probability sample's estimates, but it changes how much one sample can be trusted. The one catch is ratios such as user's accuracy: from only two or three squares they lean, as Step 3 shows. Eating whole squares gets worse, and spreading bites across the pizza gets better. Turn the two dials and watch it happen.

What Sections 2.4 and 4.7 say

Correlation changes precision, never bias

Estimates and their variance formulas stay unbiased however strong the spatial correlation is (Sec. 2.4, citing De Gruijter and Ter Braak 1990). There's no need to space bites apart to make the maths valid. Turn both dials all the way and the piles in Step 3 still centre on the truth.

Neighbours agree, so clusters cost precision

Positive correlation raises the standard errors of cluster samples, because the spots inside a cluster are alike (Sec. 2.4). Section 2.2 adds that each cluster may be worth only a modest number of separate points. Look at the Worth column for whole squares.

stratify for objectives, cluster for costs

Spread out, and it gets better

The same correlation makes a systematic sample more precise than a simple random one. That's why a spatially well spread sample is design criterion 7 (Sec. 2.2). One unit per spatial stratum is a simple alternative to a grid (Sec. 2.4). The catch with one bite per square is criterion 6: there's no unbiased variance formula.

Don't space bites by hand

Drawing a random sample and then moving or throwing out bites that land close together makes the chances of each spot very hard to work out. Without those chances it isn't a probability sample any more (Sec. 2.4). Use a documented design instead, like the grid or the squares here.

The matrix can't say where

An error matrix is a summary of the whole map (Sec. 4.7). Accuracy for parts of the map needs a large sample. Methods that predict accuracy at unsampled places lean on spatial correlation, and real reference samples are often too sparse for them.
